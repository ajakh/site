What Is a Brute Force Attack?

Brute Force Attack Definition

A brute force attack is when a hacker tries lots of different passwords and usernames until they find the right ones to get into someone's account or a company's computer system. They use computers to quickly try out loads of combinations until they get it right. It's called "brute force" because it's like trying to break down a door by hitting it hard over and over. Even though it's been around for a while, hackers still like using brute force attacks because they can be pretty effective.

Different Ways of Brute Force Attacks

A Basic Brute Force Attack

A basic brute force attack happens when a hacker tries to guess someone's login details without using special software. They might try common passwords like "password123" or simple PIN codes. These attacks work because many people still use weak passwords or reuse them across different sites. Sometimes, hackers don't need fancy tools; they just use simple clues like someone's favorite sports team to guess their password.

Dictionary Attacks

Dictionary attacks are like a simpler version of brute force hacking. The attacker picks a target and tries out different passwords with that person's username, going through lists of words, sometimes adding numbers or symbols. It takes time and usually doesn't work as well as newer hacking methods.

Hybrid Brute Force Attacks

In a hybrid brute force attack, the hacker blends a dictionary attack with a basic brute force approach. First, they have a username and use a dictionary to try common words. Then, they apply brute force by testing various character combinations. This mix helps them find passwords like "SanDiego123" or "Rover2020."

Reverse Brute Force Attack

In a reverse brute force attack, the hacker starts with a known password, often obtained from a network breach, then checks millions of usernames to find one that matches. Sometimes they even try a common weak password like "Password123" across a database of usernames.

Credential Stuffing

Credential stuffing takes advantage of people's lax password habits. Hackers gather stolen username and password pairs and try them on different websites to break into more accounts. This works when people use the same login details across multiple sites.

Why Hackers Do It

Brute force hacking demands patience — it could stretch over months or even years. Yet the potential gains are immense.

Manipulate Ads or User Activity

A hacker might conduct brute force attacks to gain financial benefits through advertising commissions by embedding spam ads on well-known websites, redirecting traffic to illicit ad-serving sites, or compromising a website with spyware that monitors user activity and sells the data to advertisers without users' permission.

Acquiring Personal Data

Through hacking, attackers can gain a wealth of sensitive information — financial records, bank accounts, private medical data. By infiltrating an account, they can impersonate individuals, siphon funds, sell credentials to others, or orchestrate broader cyber assaults.

Malware Spread

Some hackers aim to cause chaos by disseminating malware through emails or SMS, hiding malware within fake websites resembling legitimate ones, or directing visitors to malicious sites. Once malware infects a user's computer, the attacker can exploit it to infiltrate interconnected systems and launch broader cyber assaults.

Take Over Systems for Harmful Purposes

Brute force attacks can be a stepping stone for malicious actors orchestrating larger attacks using numerous devices, forming what's known as a botnet — often used for distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks aimed at overwhelming the target's security measures.

Destroy a Company's Reputation

Brute force attacks are frequently initiated to extract data from an organization, resulting in financial losses and significant reputational harm. Websites may also be targeted with attacks that inundate them with inappropriate content.

Brute Force Attack Tools

Hackers have created software and tools to assist in breaking passwords. Commonly used tools include Aircrack-ng, a set of tools designed to evaluate Wi-Fi network security, and John the Ripper, an open-source password retrieval tool that supports numerous cipher and hash types including user passwords for macOS, Unix, and Windows, database servers, web applications, and encrypted files.

Brute force attacks often require substantial computing power, so hackers combine a device's CPU with its GPU to execute multiple tasks simultaneously, significantly accelerating password cracking.

How to Prevent Brute Force Attacks

Stronger Password Practices

  • Create complex passwords exceeding 10 characters with a mix of uppercase, lowercase, symbols, and digits.
  • Use intricate passphrases — multiple words interspersed with special characters.
  • Steer clear of common passwords like names, sports teams, or "password."
  • Never recycle passwords across multiple platforms.
  • Use a password manager to generate and organize secure, unique passwords automatically.

Technical Safeguards

  • Employ high encryption rates such as 256-bit to safeguard system passwords.
  • Implement hash salting to fortify and shield password hashes.
  • Embrace multi-factor authentication (MFA) so a stolen password alone can't open an account.
  • Restrict login attempts — locking out further tries after a few unsuccessful attempts.
  • Integrate CAPTCHA into the login process to impede automated attacks.
  • Deploy IP blacklists to prevent access from known malicious addresses.
  • Eliminate unused accounts, especially those with elevated access privileges.

Encryption Keys Explained

An encryption key transforms data into a seemingly random string of characters, rendering it unintelligible without the correct key. A 128-bit encryption key requires exploring two to the power of 128 combinations to decode — practically impossible for even the most powerful computers. Many websites and browsers use 128-bit encryption as a baseline, while 256-bit encryption offers protection so strong that even a computer processing trillions of combinations per second couldn't breach it through brute force.

Brute Force FAQs

Is a brute force attack illegal?

Yes. In most cases, conducting a brute force attack without authorization is illegal and considered a form of unauthorized access or hacking.

How common are brute force attacks?

They remain prevalent, targeting various systems and networks. Frequency fluctuates based on security measures in place and evolving cyber threats.

How long to crack an eight-character password?

Depending on the complexity and the attacker's computational power, anywhere from minutes to centuries.

How are brute force attacks detected?

Common detection methods include monitoring for abnormal login attempts, analyzing unusual access patterns, rate limiting, behavioral analysis, IP blacklisting, CAPTCHA challenges, and reviewing authentication logs for suspicious activity.
